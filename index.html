    Case study · Web · team project

    NYPL Events redesign

    People could find New York Public Library events, but not always the confidence to go. Research into how they actually plan led to eight changes, from new filters to clearer RSVPs.

    Role
    UX research and design
    Team
    Team project
    Scope
    Homepage, event listings, filters and event pages
    Methods
    Card sorting, tree testing, interviews, usability tests
    6research methods
    3competitors compared
    8design changes

    01 · The problem

    Finding an event wasn't the hard part

    People could discover events on the NYPL site, but it didn't always give them the information or the confidence they needed to decide whether an event was relevant or worth attending.

    Along the way they ran into confusing filters, inconsistent navigation, information-heavy pages and unclear registration actions.

    02 · Research

    People plan around their own lives

    • People think about their own plans

      They looked for events by personal circumstance: what's on this weekend, what's nearby, what they could go to with friends. The site organized events by institutional categories.

    • Practical details decide

      Location, time, accessibility and registration requirements mattered most when deciding whether to attend.

    03 · Process

    Six ways of asking

    1. Card sorting

      How people group events in their heads.

    2. Tree testing

      Whether they could find things in the structure.

    3. User interviews

      How they decide what to go to.

    4. Paper prototype testing

      Early usability tests before any polish.

    5. Competitive analysis

      Meetup, Eventbrite and Brooklyn Public Library.

    6. Iterative prototyping

      Refining the design round by round.

    04 · Solution

    Built around plans, not categories

    • Reorganized homepage content to match users' mental models
    • Rebuilt filters around location, time, accessibility, topic and age group
    • Added time-of-day filters and map-based location selection
    • Supported multiple filters with clearer active filter states
    • Improved navigation with breadcrumbs and consistent sidebar behavior
    • Simplified event cards to highlight essential information
    • Made RSVP buttons more visible and consistent
    • Added calendar integration and related event recommendations

    05 · Reflection

    Confidence is part of usability

    Usability isn't always about whether someone can find something. Sometimes the bigger problem is whether they have enough information and confidence to act on what they've found.

    What this taught me

    It also taught me to translate research findings into specific design decisions, rather than changing things based on visual preference alone.

    Case study · Web · accessibility research

    Amon Carter Museum accessibility

    How do visitors with disabilities use the Amon Carter Museum of American Art's website to plan a visit? We evaluated what gets in their way before they ever arrive.

    Role
    Accessibility research
    Team
    Team project
    Scope
    Homepage and Plan Your Visit
    Focus
    WCAG evaluation, inclusive design, pre-visit information
    7research methods and checks
    2key insights
    5design implications

    01 · The problem

    Accessibility starts before the visit

    Accessibility isn't limited to physical accommodations inside a building. Visitors also need accessible, accurate and detailed information to know whether a museum can meet their needs.

    Planning can mean researching accessible entrances, transportation, seating, restrooms, sensory conditions, navigation and available accommodations. When that information is missing, vague or hard to find, visitors face more uncertainty and more planning work.

    02 · Research

    General statements aren't enough

    • Accessibility isn't one category

      Museums often present accessibility as a single broad topic, even though visitors have very different needs.

    • Broad statements leave people guessing

      General accessibility statements often don't give someone enough detail to know whether a specific space or experience will work for them.

    03 · Process

    Checking the site, then the information

    1. Desk research

      Accessibility barriers in museums.

    2. Museum review

      How museums communicate accessibility.

    3. WCAG spot checks

      Color use, non-text contrast, keyboard access, text resizing, motion and auto-updating content.

    4. Content review

      How clear and organized the pre-visit information is.

    04 · Design implications

    Specific, findable, independent

    • Make accessibility information specific, not generalized
    • Let visitors find relevant accommodations without digging through unrelated pages
    • Support independent decision-making
    • Integrate accessibility into the whole visitor experience, not a separate corner
    • Treat technical accessibility and accessible information as equally important

    05 · Reflection

    More than a checklist

    Accessibility isn't simply a checklist of technical requirements. It's also about whether people have the information and resources they need to participate.

    What this taught me

    I'm especially interested in how small information gaps can create big barriers for people with different abilities and needs.

    Case study · Mobile app · concept

    Pounce: find shop cats

    A mobile app concept for discovering the cats who live in, or regularly visit, local bodegas, bookstores, cafés and other neighborhood spots. It's built around trust, recent sightings and community.

    Role
    End-to-end UX
    Team
    Team project
    Platform
    Mobile app concept
    Focus
    Community content, trust, local discovery
    4competitor apps compared
    3key insights
    7features explored

    01 · The problem

    Shop cats are hard to find

    People love running into shop cats, but there's no easy, reliable way to find them. Information is scattered across social media, maps, individual posts and word of mouth, and it's often outdated, incomplete or hard to verify.

    We started with the lack of a central, reliable way to find shop cats. As the research grew, it opened up bigger questions about local discovery, user-generated content, community participation and trust.

    02 · Research

    Trust has to be earned

    • Trust has to be earned

      People look for signs a recommendation is recent, accurate and backed by others, like checking the dates on Google Maps reviews.

    • Discovery spans platforms

      Someone might spot a place on social media, confirm it on Google Maps, then ask friends or online communities for more.

    • People contribute for many reasons

      Social connection, shared interests, enjoyment, recognition and wanting to help others all drive participation.

    03 · Process

    From interviews to features

    1. Screeners and interview guides

      Finding the right people to talk to.

    2. Qualitative interviews

      Why people share local information, how they judge credibility and how they check it's current.

    3. Competitive analysis

      Catmaps NYC, Cat Collector, ShopCats and Whisker Tracker.

    4. Synthesis

      Themes, insights and needs, then user profiles and journey maps.

    5. Design

      Concepts, mobile interface features and a visual identity.

    04 · Features we explored

    Playful, but accurate

    • Interactive cat map of shop cats by neighborhood
    • Cat profiles with photos, locations and community info
    • Community sightings with photos and updates
    • Recent sightings that show whether a cat is likely still there
    • Cat-themed navigation: a cat compass, map markers and paw-shaped controls
    • Community challenges like cat scavenger hunts, cat bingo and photo challenges
    • Leaderboards and recognition for contributors

    The key design challenge: balancing the playful, emotional fun of finding cats with the practical need for accurate information and easy navigation.

    05 · Reflection

    Why people share

    Rather than assuming people would contribute just because they love cats, we needed to understand what motivates sharing, what makes community information trustworthy, and how to create value for both contributors and seekers.

    What this taught me

    Pounce brings together a lot of what I care about: cats, community, local discovery, playful design and human behavior.
